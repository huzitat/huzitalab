The Forest Environmental Assessment Laboratory studies how forests and wood create value for people, regions, and the environment.

Our work is based on the idea that forests should not be evaluated only as ecosystems or natural resources. Forests also create environmental, economic, social, and cultural value.

We aim to make these values visible and to explore how they can support sustainable forest use, regional economies, and renewed relationships between people and forests.

Our Mission

Our mission is to evaluate the value of forests and wood from multiple perspectives.

We study:

  • the environmental performance of wood and forest utilization;
  • the economic impacts of forestry and wood use on local and regional economies;
  • the social and cultural values that connect people with satoyama forests;
  • the conditions needed to return forests to regional cycles of everyday life and resource use.

Integrating Natural and Social Sciences

Many forest-related issues cannot be understood through natural science or social science alone.

For example, the ecological value of a satoyama forest may depend on soil conditions, fungi, vegetation, and biodiversity. At the same time, whether local residents return to the forest may depend on economic incentives, cultural meaning, local knowledge, and social relationships.

For this reason, our laboratory combines methods from both fields.

Our research includes environmental assessment, economic analysis, forest and soil surveys, DNA and fungal analysis, GIS, interviews, questionnaire surveys, and collaborative research with local communities.

Creating Value from Forests

Wood can have environmental advantages over materials such as concrete and steel.

Locally produced wood may also generate employment, income, and economic activity within a region.

Satoyama forests may regain meaning through resources such as mushrooms, biodiversity, landscape value, local knowledge, and opportunities for community participation.

We investigate how these different forms of value can be measured, communicated, and used to create more sustainable relationships between forests and society.

Collaboration

We welcome collaboration with:

  • students and researchers;
  • local residents and community groups;
  • forestry and wood-related businesses;
  • public organizations and local governments;
  • schools and educational institutions;
  • anyone interested in forests, wood utilization, and regional sustainability.
